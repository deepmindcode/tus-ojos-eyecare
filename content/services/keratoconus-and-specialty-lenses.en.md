# Keratoconus and specialty lenses

Some corneas are not regularly shaped. When that happens, ordinary lenses do not correct well no matter how carefully the prescription is refined: light enters distorted and the image never quite comes into focus.

That is what specialty lens designs are for.

### Who it is for

* You have been diagnosed with **keratoconus**.
* You have an **irregular cornea** from previous surgery, scarring or a transplant.
* You see halos, glare or double images even with your glasses on.
* Your prescription shifts a lot and quickly, and you are told something different every year.
* You tried regular contact lenses and cannot tolerate them.

### What gets fitted

* **Scleral lenses** — they rest on the sclera, the white of the eye, instead of on the cornea. They hold a chamber of fluid over the eye that creates a new, regular optical surface. Usually the most comfortable option when the cornea is very irregular.
* **Tricurve and other specialty designs** — geometries made to sit on corneas that are not spherical.
* **Combinations** — when it makes sense to split the work between a contact lens and glasses.

### What to expect

Fitting these lenses is not like buying ordinary contacts. It takes more than one visit, and that is normal.

1. **The actual shape of your cornea is measured,** not just your prescription.
2. **A diagnostic lens is tried** and how it sits is observed.
3. **The geometry is adjusted** until it rests where it should and you see comfortably.
4. **You are taught to insert, remove and care for them,** which with these lenses has its own technique.
5. **You are rechecked after days and weeks,** because real comfort is measured in use.

### Frequently asked questions

**Do they hurt?**
They should not. A well-fitted scleral lens rests on an area with very little sensitivity. Discomfort is a sign that it needs adjusting.

**How long does the fitting take?**
Several visits over a few weeks. It is a process, not a purchase.

**Are they useful if I have already had surgery?**
Often yes, and that is one of the cases where they help most. Bring your surgical report.

**Does insurance cover them?**
Some plans cover medically necessary contact lenses, which is different from cosmetic ones. Check with your office before the appointment.
